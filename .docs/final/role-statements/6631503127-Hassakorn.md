## Reflection

Working as the Survey & QA Lead on the Boxer Recovery App project taught me that building a technically sound recovery engine is only half the battle; how athletes actually experience and trust the interface determines whether the product succeeds. One of the most revealing moments during this project was seeing the contrast in our evaluation data. While our proxy testers achieved a 91% task success rate and rated the app’s usefulness highly (TAM PU of 4.25/5), our System Usability Scale score landed at 56.9 out of 100—well below our target of 68. Analyzing the open-ended feedback alongside those numbers showed me why: text-heavy screens, inconsistent Thai translations, and unclear button cues created friction that slowed daily input down to an average of 120 seconds, missing our 90-second goal.

This gap between functionality and usability was my biggest takeaway. Without structured SUS and TAM surveys paired with qualitative notes, our team might have assumed that a high task completion rate meant the app was ready. Instead, the data gave us clear, evidence-backed priorities for the next iteration: adding intuitive icons, fixing localization, simplifying button hierarchy, and exploring AI-assisted guidance to make daily decisions faster.

At the same time, relying on proxy testers rather than active professional boxers was a clear limitation. Simulated testing helped us catch major UI and workflow issues early, but it could not fully replicate the physical fatigue, time pressure, or fight-camp habits of real athletes. If I were to run this phase again, I would conduct a brief pilot test on the Thai copy and layout before full UAT, and schedule earlier recruitment with real fighters to validate whether our recovery rules hold up under real training conditions. Overall, this role strengthened my ability to turn raw user feedback into concrete engineering decisions and showed me the practical value of disciplined QA in software development.
