## What I Owned

As Product Owner, I was responsible for **what** the team built and in what order. I defined the problem — boxers in fight camp get one general readiness score that cannot tell them what is safe to train today — and set the product goals and scope: six separate recovery domains, a Safety Gate before any advice, and under 90 seconds of daily input. I kept the scope realistic for a prototype, for example no calorie counting and no machine learning.

I owned the Product Backlog: user stories with acceptance criteria, prioritised P0–P3 and mapped to MoSCoW, with the MUST stories marked. I represented the user's needs to the team, made sure PDPA and safety rules were part of the requirements, and checked finished work against the acceptance criteria.

I also owned the testing evidence. Because we could not test with real boxers, I documented the PROXY usability test (8 users, tasks T1–T7), recalculated the SUS results, wrote the no-real-user test report, built the issue log, and corrected the case study and comparison table so that every number matches measured data. From these results I set the next priorities: training/nutrition entry (I-01) and Thai translation (I-02).

## Reflection

Building this app with AI changed how I think about software engineering. As Product Owner, I learned that AI can help turn a broad problem into user stories, acceptance criteria, and draft documentation quickly. That made early requirements work easier to share with the team and gave us more time to discuss priorities. The benefit only mattered when we checked the output against the real scope. Our project changed during development: for example, the delivered app uses a rule-based recovery engine and labelled FightCamp mock sessions, not runtime machine learning or a live FightCamp integration. Keeping those distinctions clear in the backlog, design, and presentation took careful teamwork. AI suggestions could sound complete while relying on assumptions, so we had to verify claims and avoid presenting goals as measured results.

One thing that got better was the speed of organizing evidence. I used AI to help format session logs and recalculate SUS scores, then checked the results against the original responses. One thing that got worse was the risk of trusting polished drafts too early: inaccurate or generic details can spread into requirements and reports unless people review them. Testing also taught me that eight proxy participants can reveal usability problems, but cannot establish whether professional boxers will find the app useful in camp. Quality and security need to be considered from the start; for this project, that meant respecting consent and sensitive health data, and keeping safety rules understandable and testable. AI helped with tasks, but communication and decisions still belonged to the team.

I see myself today as an early-career software engineer in an industry where AI makes implementation faster, while increasing the value of people who can define the right problem, judge evidence, and take responsibility for a product. I want to use AI as an assistant while strengthening my own engineering judgment.

Next year, I plan to build practical experience through an internship or entry-level role, improve my skills in requirements, testing, and product analysis, and learn from users and experienced teammates. In five years, I hope to be a product-focused engineer or product owner who can lead a team from a validated user need to a safe, reliable release—and explain the evidence behind its decisions.
